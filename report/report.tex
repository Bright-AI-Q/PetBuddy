%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
   There are approximately 200 - 350 breeds of dogs and 45 - 70 breeds of cats. Different types of breeds can have different care instructions. Thus, it can be difficult for pet owners to keep track of the best ways to care for their pets. This project aims to create an application that will generate breed specific pet care instructions based on an image of the pet. We introduce our own multiclass pet breed classifier called PetNet, which is based on MobileNetV2's architecture~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}. We aim for this to be a lightweight network that could be used in resource constrained environments such as mobile devices. We also introduce a novel data augmentation method for images named LDRE. For the language model component, we utilize Qwen~\cite{bai2023qwen} to generate breed specific pet care instructions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction/Background/Motivation}
\subsection{Problem}
The main problem we are trying to solve is the multiclass classification of pet breeds in resource constrained environments such as mobile phones. Classifying pet breeds is a challenging problem because there are many similarities between breeds. The differences between breeds are very subtle, often relying on differences such as ear position or snout shape. Oftentimes, the appearance of the same breed could appear wildly different due to fur pattern and color, as well as the grooming of the pet. We will try to create a deep learning model.

\subsection{Current practice}
The current literature on approaches to this problem is focused on larger neural network architectures. In 2019, Borwarnginn et al. compared the performance of conventional approaches based on Local Binary Pattern (LBP) and Histogram of Oriented Gradient (HOG), as well as the deep learning based approach using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) with transfer learning~\cite{8929955}. In this paper, they worked on a subset of the Columbia dogs with parts dataset~\cite{10.1007/978-3-642-33718-5_13}. They selected 20/133 dog breeds with the constraint that each breed has more than 60 images. For each breed, 40 images are used for training and 20 images are used for testing. They were able to achieve 96.75\% accuracy using CNNs compared to 79.25\% using HOG descriptors.

In 2021, the same group of authors attempted the problem again~\cite{borwarnginn2021knowing} with the entire Columbia dogs with parts dataset~\cite{10.1007/978-3-642-33718-5_13} instead of a subset of it. This time, the authors evaluated the performance of 3 different pretrained CNNs with various data augmentation settings. The 3 CNN architectures used are NASNet~\cite{qin2019nasnet}, InceptionV3~\cite{szegedy2016rethinking}, and MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}. The authors were able to achieve the highest accuracy of 89.92\% using the NASNet architecture.

\subsubsection{Limits}
Inference time and hardware requirements make the current practice infeasible for local inference in mobile devices. In our paper, we attempt to build on top of the MobileNetV2 architecture since our goal is to run local inference on resource constrained devices such as mobile phones with reasonable inference times.

\subsection{Target audience}
Our target audience for this application are pet owners who want to take better care of their pets. We aim for this application to be simple to use such that the user doesn't need to scour the web for breed specific care instructions.

From a deep learning perspective, this project tackles a few different problems. That is, fine grained image classification, small models running on resource constrained hardware, and LLM training with limited hardware. If we are successful, we would be able to better understand these problems and improve current approaches.

\subsection{Dataset}
\subsubsection{Classification dataset}
We used a combination of the Stanford dogs dataset~\cite{khosla2011novel} and the Oxford pets dataset~\cite{6248092}. The Stanford dogs dataset includes 20,580 annotated images of dogs belonging to 120 breeds. Each image is annotated with a bounding box and a class label. Each breed contains 150-200 images. The Oxford pets dataset, on the other hand, includes 7390 images of cats and dogs of 37 different breeds. 25 are dogs and 12 are cats. The Oxford dogs dataset contains about 200 images for each breed.

Our combined dataset contains 27,970 annotated images of cats and dogs belonging to 144 breeds. Of these, 136 are dogs and 12 are cats. The number of cat breeds is much less than that of dogs, but each cat breed contains 150-200 images, so it is still sufficient for our vision model to learn a good representation for cats. We then split the images into training, validation, and test set in a 8:1:1 ratio. We will name this our composite dataset for pet classification.

\subsubsection{Pose dataset}
The Ap-10k~\cite{yu2021ap}, Animal Pose~\cite{Cao_2019_ICCV}, and StanfordExtra dog~\cite{biggs2020left} datasets are used to finetune YOLOv8-pose~\cite{yolov8_ultralytics}. This pose model uses pretrained weights provided by Ultralytics to accurately detect 5 keypoints of pets. That is, left eye, right eye, left ear, right ear, and nose. This will be used in our Landmark-Driven Region Erasing (LDRE) method, which is a data augmentation method that will be further elaborated in the next section. This will be our composite dataset for animal pose.

\subsubsection{Pet knowledge database}
For the pet knowledge database used for the LLM part, the data is scraped from pet breed information websites~\cite{dog-breeds-net},~\cite{wisdom-panel-cat}. This data is organized into individual file for each breed and further divided into subsections based on the relevant headings. The pet knowledge database contains information on general breed overview, care instructions, grooming, exercise, health, temperament, family suitability, training, and diet. Data missing in these categories are added using Gemini 2.5~\cite{gemini2.5}. Test dataset is created by scraping Q\&A content from online pet-care websites~\cite{everypaw-dog}~\cite{everypaw-cat} not included in the LLM’s training corpus. The dataset contains 100 questions with corresponding model answers for randomly sampled breeds.

\section{Approach}

We separate our project into 2 parts. First, the vision model, where we perform multi-class classification and assign one label to the image. Second, the language model, where we generate pet care instructions based on the specific breed of the pet. We aim to solve the problem by applying various techniques from previous research to improve validation accuracy of the model. We think it will be successful because the majority of these techniques are from well known papers with promising results when applied to other domains. In addition to this, we introduce a new data augmentation technique called Landmark-Driven Region Erasing (LDRE).

\subsection{Vision model}
We use MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} as the backbone architecture with pretrained weights as our starting network. We then added several modifications to the architecture in an attempt to improve it. For a particular image, object detection is first done using YOLOv8 pretrained weights~\cite{10533619} provided by Ultralytics without finetuning to generate bounding boxes around cats and dogs. Then, the bounding boxes that contain cats or dogs will be fed to our custom PetNet architecture to perform classification.

\subsubsection{Dual attention mechanism}
2 attention mechanisms are added after the Inverted Residual Block (IRB) layers in the original MobileNetV2 architecture. First, Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) from ECA-net~\cite{Wang_2020_CVPR} is used as the first attention mechanism. Second, learnable 2D positional encodings introduced by DEtection TRansformers (DETR)~\cite{carion2020end} are added to our image features. To handle varying input resolutions without training, the dynamic interpolation strategy introduced in Vision Transformers (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2020image} is used to resize our learnable positional encodings to match the current dimensions of the image features.

\subsubsection{Self Knowledge Distillation (KD)}
A self KD strategy inspired by Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2019your} is implemented, where auxiliary heads at intermediate stages are trained to mimic the final classification layer's soft prediction. Auxiliary classifiers are added to stage 2 and stage 3 of the MobileNetV2 architecture, which acts as a student. The last classifier (stage 4) acts as the teacher. We compute this loss as the KL divergence between the student and the teacher logits with the following equation. This loss is added to our cross entropy loss for classification.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{KD} = \sum_{i \in \{2,3\}} \alpha_i \cdot T^2 \cdot \mathcal{KL} \left( \sigma \left( \frac{z_t}{T} \right), \sigma \left( \frac{z_{s_i}}{T} \right) \right)
\end{equation}
Where $i$ is the index of the stage starting from 1, $\alpha_i$ is the weight of the loss of the stage, $T$ is the temperature, which is a scalar used to scale the output probabilities, $z_t$ are the teacher's logits, and $z_{s_i}$ are the student's logits.

\subsubsection{Landmark-Driven Random Erasing (LDRE)}
This is a new data augmentation technique devised by Bright Wang built on top of Random Erasing~\cite{zhong2020random}. The Grid-based dropping mechanism is inspired by GridMask~\cite{chen2020gridmask}. This method is similar to the one introduced by Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2017hide} but instead of random grid masking, LDRE explicitly targets semantic regions such as the head and body to force the model to not rely too heavily on certain semantic features of the model. Instead of random occlusion, LDRE performs semantic occlusion.

Ultralytics~\cite{yolov8_ultralytics} pretrained weights for YOLOv8-pose is used to identify 5 key anatomical keypoints of pets: left eye, right eye, left ear, right ear, and nose. However, YOLOv8-pose is initially trained to identify keypoints in humans instead of dogs and cats. This leads to low accuracy in identifying keypoints in dogs and cats. That is why we finetuned the YOLOv8-pose model with the composite dataset for animal pose.

First, the image is divided into a 16x16 fixed size grid. Then, we calculate the score of each cell based on different criteria. For standard LDRE, our scoring function is the number of keypoints present in a cell, regardless of the keypoint's confidence. For enhanced LDRE, a saliency score $S_{i,j}$ is calculated for each grid cell based on keypoint confidence and whether the keypoint exists in the particular grid cell, as shown in the following equation.

\begin{equation}
    S_{i,j} = \sum_{k \in K} c_k \cdot \text{dist}(k, \text{cell}_{i,j})
\end{equation}
Where $i$ is the index of the row, $j$ is the index of the column, $k$ is the particular keypoint in the set of keypoints $K$, $c_k$ is the confidence of the keypoint, specifically the probability associated with detecting the keypoint from our pose model. $cell_{i,j}$ is the center of the particular grid. Currently, the $dist$ function determines whether the keypoint belongs to a particular cell, outputting 1 if it is and 0 otherwise.

Next, we identify the top-k highest scoring cells and mask them. The idea is that this method acts as a regularizer and forces the model to learn from the patterns of the body, tail, and fur rather than just the face.

An example of LDRE can be seen in figure~\ref{fig:ldre_example}. The enhanced version does the same masking with Gaussian noise, but with different selection criteria of cells.

\subsubsection{Data augmentation}
CutMix~\cite{yun2019cutmix} and MixUp~\cite{zhang2017mixup} will be randomly applied with a 50\% chance during training to act as a regularizer and improve the generalization of the model.

\subsection{Language model}
There are two approaches explored to improve LLM performance in the pet knowledge domain. First, fine-tuning of the Qwen backbone~\cite{bai2023qwen} using QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}. Second, implement a RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)~\cite{lewis2020rag} by accessing the relevant information in the pet knowledge database during the generation process.

\subsubsection{Fine-tuning of Qwen using QLoRA}

The training data are model question-answer pairs generated from the pet knowledge database. For each information section, 2 to 3 questions are generated. We also tried to generate the dataset for cats by giving the questions to Gemini and taking its responses as the model answers. Each data sample consists of a single question as the "instruction", the breed name as the "input", and the corresponding database information as the "output". In total, the generated conversation dataset contains 2187 training samples and 548 validation samples. This dataset is used to train Qwen.

\subsubsection{RAG implementation}
Regex is used to extract the breed name from the question prompt. The contents of the database for the corresponding breed are loaded and included in the LLM prompt. By storing the information for each breed in a different file and organizing the content into sectioned headings, the LLM can quickly find relevant information.

\subsection{Problems}
The problems we anticipate are the performance of smaller neural network models. Smaller neural network models might not achieve high validation accuracy, which would decrease the usability of this application. Another problem we anticipate would be whether adding different modules to our model would actually lead to an increase in validation accuracy.

The problems we encountered are mainly related to hardware resources. Training the model with L40S GPU on the PACE ICE cluster still took 1 hour for each training run. Testing the different configurations took a lot of time. Some of the modules that we added did not appear to have an impact on validation accuracy at first glance, such as the addition of LDRE to MixUp. However, with a different approach to train the model (curriculum learning~\cite{bengio2009curriculum}), we were able to achieve an increase in validation accuracy. This will be elaborated on in the experiments section.

The first thing we tried, which is simply using MobileNetV2's pretrained backbone did work, as it provided a good baseline for our model. We then tried several different techniques to improve the existing MobileNetV2 backbone.

\subsection{Vision model}
We measured success by comparing the validation accuracy of our vision model (PetNet) to 3 different baselines. We aim to have a high validation accuracy while keeping the number of parameters of our model small. We experimented with different configurations of our model to determine which configuration has the highest validation accuracy. Our final configuration (curriculum learning) has 3.06 million learnable parameters. The loss function we used was cross-entropy with the model's predicted label probabilities compared with the actual label. We compare the validation accuracy of PetNet compared to other neural network architectures in table~\ref{tab:petnet}. In general, we would consider this project a success.

\subsubsection{Comparison with baselines}
Pretrained weights for MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, EfficientNet-BO~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}, and MobileOne-S0~\cite{vasu2023mobileone} with finetuning are used as our baseline for comparison. The weights for MobileNetV2 were acquired from torchvision~\cite{marcel2010torchvision}. The weights for the other 2 models were acquired from PyTorch Image Models (timm)~\cite{rw2019timm}. These models are finetuned on our composite dataset for pet classification with the same hyperparameters. The performance of this baseline can be seen in table~\ref{tab:petnet}.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter tuning}
The Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna} scripts were run on the PACE cluster and a local RTX-3060 for training. The tuned parameters are learning rate (1e-6 - 1e-2), weight decay (1e-6 - 1e-2), and optimizer (AdamW / SGD). These hyperparameters are kept the same for different configurations to ensure a fair comparison.

\subsubsection{Dual attention}
The addition of attention mechanisms increased validation accuracy. As seen in figure~\ref{fig:attn_train_loss}, training loss decreases smoothly and steadily. As shown in figure~\ref{fig:attn_val_loss}, the validation loss also decreases steadily, although not as smoothly as the training loss curve. There is no underfitting since both loss curves are decreasing steadily and the loss values are not high. There is no overfitting since the training loss did not continue to decrease while the validation loss plateaus. The validation accuracy in figure~\ref{fig:attn_val_acc} is also steadily increasing, surpassing the baseline around epoch 65. Intuitively, this might be due to attention layers increasing the capacity of the model. This enables the model to generalize better to unseen validation data.

\subsubsection{Self KD}
Next, we tried adding self KD to act as a regularizer and to improve the model's ability to generalize. As seen in figure~\ref{fig:attn_train_loss}, compared to the training loss curve for attention only, adding self KD increases the training loss, although the curve still decreases steadily. This makes sense because self KD makes training harder with a regularizing effect. As seen in figure~\ref{fig:attn_val_loss}, the curve looks similar to the one with attention only, but the validation loss is lower after adding self KD. This correlates to our validation accuracy curve in figure~\ref{fig:attn_val_acc}, where the 2 curves look similar, but ultimately adding self KD increased the model's validation accuracy. In this case, no underfitting or overfitting is observed. This led to a great increase in validation accuracy of 9.28\% compared to the baseline, as shown in table~\ref{tab:petnet}.

Both standard and enhanced LDRE is then added to our model in order to experiment with this new method. \subsubsection{Curriculum learning}
In our previous section, we found that combining MixUp / FixMatch and LDRE did not have much impact on validation accuracy. Therefore, we experimented with a curriculum learning approach~\cite{bengio2009curriculum}. We train the first 100 epochs with attention, self KD, and MixUp. Then, we train the remaining 20 epochs with attention, self KD, and enhanced LDRE (MixUp is disable). In practice, we finetuned from the best model configuration we get so far with the attention, self KD, and MixUp setup, then we run finetuning with MixUp disabled and enhanced LDRE enabled. From figure~\ref{fig:curriculum_jump}, we can see that we are able to get a 2.34\% jump in validation accuracy compared to the MixUp only setup. Compared to popular baseline models, our final setup is able to achieve a validation accuracy of 83.69\%. Refer to figure~\ref{fig:sota_comparison} to see a comparison of our model to popular baselines.

\subsection{Language Model}
For our evaluation of language model performance, we used the LLM-as-a-judge method~\cite{zheng2023judging} with Gemini 2.5 Flash~\cite{gemini2.5}. Gemini is asked to rate each prompt on a score of 1 - 5 based on 3 criteria: breed consistency, medical safety, and usefulness (max score = 15). The scores of each configuration are shown in table~\ref{tab:llm_performance}.

The base + RAG variant is the most successful with the highest win rate compared to the base model. The fine-tuned model saw a couple of major score drops when it outputs the wrong information, or sometimes even the information of another breed. This is indicative of the LLM not being able to retain specific information about each breed distinctly. This may be due to the LLM's small parameters size not being able to represent the full range of information. Trying out more training epochs resulted in the model's performance dropping, suggesting overfitting. Furthermore, there are flaws in the training dataset itself. It is too small and contains too little variation in phrasings and sentences. Since conversations are automatically created from sections of the pet knowledge database, the model answer often contains extraneous data that are not needed to answer the question. This results in the fine-tuned model's answers being long-winded and including irrelevant information. Interestingly, the fine-tuned model with RAG performs slightly worse than the base LLM with RAG. Generally, it is given fewer points than the base model + RAG variant because of its long-winded answers. Overall, breed specific pet knowledge contains complex and highly specific data, which makes it more suited for RAG implementation than the fine-tuning method.

\subsection{Sample Language Model Outputs}
\textbf{Question}: Do Gordon Setters need a lot of grooming?

\subsubsection{Reference Answer}
Gordon Setters have a beautiful, dense coat that requires regular grooming to keep it in optimal condition. Their long, silky hair is prone to tangling and matting, so regular brushing is essential to prevent these issues. Aim to brush their coat at least two to three times a week using a slicker brush or a comb with wide-spaced teeth. A professional groomer can support and advise on your Gordon Setter. Like any dog, regular tooth brushing with a dog-specific toothpaste twice daily is ideal. If you can’t manage that often, just do it as often as you can

\subsubsection{Base Model}
Yes, Gordon Setters require regular grooming due to their double coat and long, thick fur. They have two layers of hair: an outer layer called the guard hairs that protect them from water and dirt, and a softer undercoat that helps insulate them in cold weather. The breed also has feathering on their legs and tails.\\
Here's what you can expect with their grooming needs:\\
1. Brushing: Regular brushing is essential to prevent tangles and mats. A good quality bristle brush or a slicker brush should be used to remove loose hair, dirt, and excess oils from their coat.\\
2. Dusting: Due to their dense undercoat, they may get dust and pollen easily. It’s important to keep them clean by giving them a gentle dust bath using a specially formulated powder or a damp cloth.\\
3. Trimming: Like other dog breeds, Gordon Setters may benefit from occasional trimming of their nails, teeth cleaning (using dental treats and chews), and any necessary ear cleaning.\\
4. Grooming for Show Dogs: For show dogs, specific grooming routines might include clipping certain areas, which can help achieve a more polished appearance. Regular grooming will not only maintain your Gordon Setter’s health but also ensure they look beautiful.\\
\textbf{AI Judge Score}: 4\\
\textbf{AI Judge Response}: The AI assistant correctly identifies the Gordon Setter's coat type but includes a medically unsafe and completely inappropriate recommendation for 'dust baths' for a dog. It also misses crucial advice on regular tooth brushing and provides vague, unhelpful information regarding show grooming, severely impacting its usefulness and safety\\

\subsubsection{Base Model + RAG}
Yes, Gordon Setters require regular grooming due to their long, silky coats. They should be brushed 3-4 times per week to prevent tangles and matting. Additionally, they need to have their feathering and ears trimmed as needed to avoid debris buildup.\\
\textbf{AI Judge Score}: 14\\
\textbf{AI Judge Response}: The AI assistant provided excellent breed-specific grooming advice, accurately detailing the coat type, brushing frequency, and the need to trim feathering and ears, which is crucial for Gordon Setters. The advice is medically safe and offers actionable steps, although it could have included details on brush types or professional grooming support for even greater usefulness, as seen in the reference.\\

\subsubsection{Fine-tuned Model}
No, they don't require extensive grooming. However, regular brushing and nail trimming help keep their coats healthy and comfortable.\\
\textbf{AI Judge Score}: 8\\
\textbf{AI Judge Response}: The AI's initial statement that Gordon Setters do not require extensive grooming is inaccurate for the breed, which has a long, dense coat needing regular care. While the advice given (brushing and nail trimming) is safe, it lacks specific actionable steps or frequency recommendations, making it minimally useful\\

\subsubsection{Fine-tuned Model + RAG}
Yes, Gordon Setters require regular grooming due to their long, silky coats. They need at least 3-4 weekly brushings to prevent tangles and matting. Additionally, trimming feathering and ear hair as needed helps maintain their healthy coat. Regular grooming ensures their beautiful black-and-tan coat stays shiny and prevents skin issues.\\
\textbf{AI Judge Score}: 11\\
\textbf{AI Judge Response}: The AI assistant's response is excellent for breed-specific coat care, accurately detailing the brushing frequency and the need for trimming feathering and ear hair. However, it completely omits crucial aspects of general dog grooming and medical safety, such as dental care and nail trims, which were included in the reference answer and are vital for a pet's overall well-being. This omission reduces its medical safety and usefulness scores despite good breed consistency
