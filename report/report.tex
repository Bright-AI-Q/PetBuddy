%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
   There are approximately 200 - 350 breeds of dogs and 45 - 70 breeds of cats. Different types of breeds can have different care instructions. Thus, it can be difficult for pet owners to keep track of the best ways to care for their pets. This project aims to create an application that will generate breed specific pet care instructions based on an image of the pet. We introduce our own multiclass pet breed classifier called PetNet, which is based on MobileNetV2's architecture~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}. We aim for this to be a lightweight network that could be used in resource constrained environments such as mobile devices. There is also a language model component, we would use Qwen~\cite{bai2023qwen} to generate breed specific pet care instructions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction/Background/Motivation}

(5 points) What did you try to do? What problem did you try to solve? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon.\\
\subsection{Problem}
The main problem we are trying to solve is multiclass pet breed classification on resource constrained environments such as mobile phones. Multiclass breed classification is a challenging problem because there are many similarities between breeds. The differences between breeds are very subtle, often relying on differences such as ear position or snout shape. Oftentimes, the appearance of the same breed could appear wildly different due to fur pattern and color, as well as the grooming of the pet. However, the current literature on the approaches to this problem is focused on larger neural network architectures. Inference time and hardware requirements make these approaches infeasible for local inference in mobile devices.

\subsection{Current practice}
(5 points) How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?\\
The problem of classifying pet breeds using a model has been attempted multiple times by different researchers. In 2012, Parkhi et al. approached dog and cat breed classification using Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) among other models and provided a comparison between them\cite{6248092}. The authors provided comprehensive reports on the performance of different models in this paper.

Similarly, in 2012, Jiongxin et al. introduced a method called parts localization in dog breeds\cite{10.1007/978-3-642-33718-5_13}. The authors built examplar-based geometric and appearance models of dog breeds and their face parts along with localizing different parts of a dog such as ears or faces in order to improve classification of the model.

Subsequently, deep learning methods became popular, and there were multiple attempts to apply deep learning methods to the classification of pet breeds. In 2019, Borwarnginn et al. compared the performance of conventional based approaches using Local Binary Pattern (LBP) and Histogram of Oriented Gradient (HOG), as well as the deep learning based approach using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) with transfer learning\cite{8929955}. In this paper, they worked on a subset of the Columbia dogs with parts dataset\cite{10.1007/978-3-642-33718-5_13}. They selected 20 out of 133 dog breeds with the constraint that each breed has more than 60 images. For each breed, 40 images are used for training and 20 images are used for testing. They were able to achieve a 96.75\% accuracy using CNNs compared to 79.25\% using HOG descriptors.

In 2021, the same group of authors attempted the problem again\cite{borwarnginn2021knowing}, but they worked on the entire Columbia dogs with parts dataset\cite{10.1007/978-3-642-33718-5_13} instead of a subset of it. This time, the authors evaluated the performance of 3 different pretrained CNNs with various data augmentation settings. The 3 CNN architectures used are NASNet\cite{qin2019nasnet}, InceptionV3\cite{szegedy2016rethinking}, and MobileNetV2\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}. The authors were able to achieve the highest accuracy of 89.92\% using the NASNet architecture. In our paper, we attempt to build on top of the MobileNetV2 architecture since our goal is to run local inference on resource constrained devices such as mobile phones.

\subsubsection{Limits}
The limits of the current practice are that the current work focuses on larger neural network architectures which are infeasible to run in a mobile device. We aim to create a model that can run local inference in a reasonable amount of time.

\subsection{Target audience}
(5 points) Who cares? If you are successful, what difference will it make?
Pet owners that care about breed specific pet care instruction would care about this problem. Some pet breeds are harder to care for and might need different environments to thrive. Before owning a pet, potential pet owners could take a photo of the pet they are interested in, then our program would generate detailed pet care instructions for the specific breed. The potential pet owners would have more information on whether they could properly care for their pet, and decide whether they are up to the task of taking good care of it.

If we are successful, we would be able to help pet owners to take better care of their pets. Similarly, we would be tackling the challenging problem of fine-grained image categorization as detailed in the original Stanford dogs paper\cite{khosla2011novel}. Solving this problem could let us understand the specific issues with fine-grained image classification in resource constrained environments.

\subsection{Dataset}

\subsubsection{Classification dataset}
We use a combination of the Stanford dogs dataset\cite{khosla2011novel} and the Oxford pets dataset\cite{6248092}. The Stanford dogs dataset includes 20,580 annotated images of dogs belonging to 120 breeds. Each image is annotated with a bounding box and object class label. Each breed contains 150-200 images. The Oxford pets dataset on the other hand includes 7390 images of cats and dogs of 37 different breeds. 25 are dogs and 12 are cats. The Oxford dogs dataset contains about 200 images for each breed.

Our combined dataset contains 27,970 annotated images of cats and dogs belonging to 144 breeds. Of those, 136 are dogs and 12 are cats. The number of cat breeds are much less than dogs, but each cat breed contains 150-200 images so it is still sufficient for our vision model to learn a good representation for cats. We then split the images into training, validation, and test set in a 8:1:1 ratio. We will name this our composite dataset for pet classification.

\subsubsection{Pose dataset}
The Ap-10k\cite{yu2021ap}, Animal Pose\cite{Cao_2019_ICCV}, and the StanfordExtra dog\cite{biggs2020left} datasets are used to finetune YOLOv8-pose pretrained weights provided by Ultralytics in order to accurately detect 5 keypoints of pets. Namely left eye, right eye, left ear, right ear, and nose. This will be used in our Landmark-Driven Region Erasing (LDRE) method which is a data augmentation method that will be further elaborated in the next section. This will be our composite dataset for animal pose.

\subsubsection{Pet knowledge database}
For the pet knowledge database used for the LLM part, the data is scraped from pet breeds information websites \cite{dog-breeds-net} \cite{wisdom-panel-cat}. This data is organized into individual file for each breed and further divided into subsections based on the relevant headings. The pet knowledge database contains information on general breed overview, care instructions, grooming, exercise, health, temperament, family suitability, training and diet. Data that are missing on these categories are added using Gemini 2.5 \cite{gemini2.5}.

\section{Approach}

(10 points) What did you do exactly? How did you solve the problem? Why did you think it would be successful? Is anything new in your approach?

We separate our project into 2 parts. First is the vision model, where we perform multi-class classification and assign one label to the image. Second is the language model, where we generate pet care instructions based on the specific breed of the pet.

\subsection{Vision model}
We use MobileNetV2\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} as the backbone architecture with pretrained weights as our starting network. We then added several modifications to the architecture in an attempt to improve it. For a particular image, object detection is first done using YOLOv8 pretrained weights\cite{10533619} provided by Ultralytics without finetuning to generate bounding boxes around cats and dogs. Then, the bounding boxes containing either cats or dogs will be fed to our custom PetNet architecture to perform classification.

\subsubsection{Dual attention mechanism}
2 attention mechanisms are added after the Inverted Residual Block (IRB) layers in the original MobileNetV2 architecture. Firstly, Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) from ECA-net introduced by Wang et al.\cite{Wang_2020_CVPR} is used as the first attention mechanism. Secondly, learnable 2D positional encodings introduced by DEtection TRansformers (DETR)\cite{carion2020end} are added to our image features. To handle varying input resolutions without training, the dynamic interpolation strategy introduced in Vision Transformers (ViT)\cite{dosovitskiy2020image} is used to resize our learnable positional encodings to match the current dimensions of the image features.

\subsubsection{Self Knowledge Distillation (KD)}
A self KD strategy inspired by Zhang et al.\cite{zhang2019your} is implemented, where auxiliary heads at intermediate stages are trained to mimic the final classification layer's soft prediction. Auxiliary classifiers are added to stage 2 and stage 3 of the MobileNetV2 architecture, which acts as the student. While the last classifier (stage 4) acts as the teacher. We compute this loss as the KL divergence between the student and the teacher logits with the following equation. This loss is added to our cross entropy loss for classification.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{KD} = \sum_{i \in \{2,3\}} \alpha_i \cdot T^2 \cdot \mathcal{KL} \left( \sigma \left( \frac{z_t}{T} \right), \sigma \left( \frac{z_{s_i}}{T} \right) \right)
\end{equation}
Where $i$ is the index of the stage starting from 1, $\alpha_i$ is the weight of the loss of the particular stage, $T$ is the temperature, which is a scalar used to scale output probabilities, $z_t$ are the teacher's logits, and $z_{s_i}$ are the student's logits.

\subsubsection{Landmark-Driven Random Erasing (LDRE)}
This is a new data augmentation technique devised by Bright Wang built on top of the Random Erasing method introduced by Zhong et al.\cite{zhong2020random}. The Grid-based dropping mechanism is inspired by GridMask\cite{chen2020gridmask}. This method is similar to the one introduced by Kumar et al.\cite{kumar2017hide} but instead of random grid masking, LDRE explicitly targets semantic regions such as the head and body to force the model to learn robust features rather than memorizing noise. Instead of random occlusion, LDRE is performing semantic occlusion.

YOLOv8-pose\cite{10533619} pretrained weights by Ultralytics are used to identify 5 key anatomical keypoints of pets: left eye, right eye, left ear, right ear, and nose. However, YOLOv8-pose is initially trained for identifying keypoints in humans instead of dogs and cats. This lead to low accuracy in identifying keypoints in dogs and cats. Which is why we finetuned the YOLOv8-pose model with the composite dataset of AP-10k\cite{yu2021ap}, Stanford Extra\cite{biggs2020left}, and the Animal Pose\cite{Cao_2019_ICCV} dataset.

First, the image is divided into a fixed size grid of 16x16. Then, we calculate the score of each cell based on different criteria. For standard LDRE, our scoring function is the number of keypoints present in a cell, regardless of the keypoint's confidence. For enhanced LDRE, a saliency score $S_{i,j}$ is calculated for each grid cell based on keypoint confidence and whether the keypoint exists in the particular grid cell as shown in the following equation.

An example of LDRE can be seen in figure \ref{fig:ldre_example}, enhanced LDRE is depicted in \ref{fig:enhanced_ldre_example}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.3\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{latex/images/ldre_example.png}
        \caption{LDRE example}
        \label{fig:ldre_example}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.3\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{latex/images/enhanced_ldre_example.png}
        \caption{Enhanced LDRE, note the ears and beginning of the legs are not masked with Gaussian noise}
        \label{fig:enhanced_ldre_example}
    \end{subfigure}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
    S_{i,j} = \sum_{k \in K} c_k \cdot \text{dist}(k, \text{cell}_{i,j})
\end{equation}
Where $i$ is the index of the row, $j$ is the index of the column, $k$ is the particular keypoint in the set of keypoints $K$, $c_k$ is the confidence of the keypoint, specifically the probability associated with detecting the keypoint from our pose model. $cell_{i,j}$ is the center of the particular grid. Currently, the $dist$ function determines whether the keypoint belongs to a particular cell, outputting 1 if it is and 0 otherwise.

Next, we identify top-k highest scoring cells and mask them. The idea is that this method acts as a regularizer and forces the model to learn from the body, tail, and fur patterns rather than just the face.

\subsubsection{Data augmentation}
CutMix\cite{yun2019cutmix} and MixUp\cite{zhang2017mixup} will be randomly applied with each having a 50\% chance during training to act as regularization and improve the generalization of the model.

\subsection{Language model}
There are two approaches explored to improve the LLM performance in the pet knowledge domain. The first is to do a fine-tuning of the backbone Qwen\cite{bai2023qwen} using QLoRA\cite{dettmers2023qlora} and the second is to implement a RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)\cite{lewis2020rag} by accessing the relevant information in the pet knowledge database during the generation process.

\subsubsection{Fine-tuning of Qwen using QLoRA}

The training data are model question-answer pairs generated from the pet knowledge database. For each information section, 2 to 3 questions are generated. We also tried to generate the dataset for cats by giving the questions to Gemini and taking its responses as the model answers. Each data sample consists of a single question as the "instruction", the breed name as the "input" and the corresponding database information as the "output". In total, the generated conversation dataset contains 2187 training samples and 548 validation samples. This dataset is used to train Qwen.

\subsubsection{RAG implementation}
Regex is used to extract the breed name from the question prompt. The contents of the database for the corresponding breed is loaded and included in the prompt to the LLM. By storing the information of each breed in a different file and organizing the content into sectioned headings, the LLM can quickly find which information is relevant.

We use Qwen\cite{bai2023qwen} as our LLM and finetune using the QLoRA\cite{dettmers2023qlora} approach.

\subsubsection{Dual attention}
The addition of attention mechanisms increased validation accuracy. As we see in figure \ref{fig:attn_train_loss}, training loss decreases smoothly and steadily. As shown in figure \ref{fig:attn_val_loss}, the validation loss also decreases steadily although not as smoothly as the training loss curve. There is no underfitting since both loss curves are decreasing steadily and loss values are not high. There is no overfitting since training loss did not continue decreasing while validation loss plateaus. The validation accuracy in figure \ref{fig:attn_val_acc} is also steadily increasing, surpassing the baseline around epoch 65. Intuitively, this might be due to attention layers increasing the ability of the model and lead the model to generalize better to unseen validation data.

\subsubsection{Self KD}
Next, we tried adding self KD to act as a regularizer and to improve the model's ability to generalize. Still looking at figure \ref{fig:attn_train_loss}, compared to the training loss curve for attention only, adding self KD increases the training loss, although the curve still decreases steadily. Which makes sense because self KD makes training harder with a regularizing effect. As seen in figure \ref{fig:attn_val_loss}, the curve looks similar to the one with attention only, but the validation loss is lower after adding self KD. This correlates to our validation accuracy curve in figure \ref{fig:attn_val_acc}, where the 2 curves look similar, but ultimately adding self KD increased the model's validation accuracy. No underfitting or overfitting is observed in this case too. This lead to a great increase in validation accuracy of 9.28\% as shown in table \ref{tab:petnet}.

\subsubsection{Curriculum learning}
In our previous section, we found that combining MixUp / FixMatch and LDRE did not have much impact to validation accuracy. Therefore, we experimented with a curriculum learning\cite{bengio2009curriculum} approach. We train the first 100 epochs with attention, self KD, and MixUp. Then, we train the remaining 20 epochs with attention, self KD, and enhanced LDRE (MixUp is disable). In practice, we finetuned from the best model configuration we get so far with the attention, self KD, and MixUp setup, then we run finetuning with MixUp disabled and enhanced LDRE enabled. From figure \ref{fig:curriculum_jump}, we can see that we are able to get a 2.34\% jump in validation accuracy compared to the MixUp only setup. Compared to popular baseline models, our final setup is able to achieve a validation accuracy of 83.69\%. Refer to figure \ref{fig:sota_comparison} to see a comparison of our model to popular baselines.
